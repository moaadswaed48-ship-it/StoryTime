# Working brief: ages 4 to 5 (group B, stories B01 to B15)

This brief stands alone. A writer, an artist or an activity designer working on a group-B story needs only this page
plus the style guide for their language. A child joins this group on the fourth birthday and leaves it on the sixth.

**How to read the tags.** Every rule carries one tag:
- **[S]** a source states it. The reference after it points into the research files: `ages §K` means
  `docs/research/ages-raw.md`, section K; `safety §1.5` means `docs/research/activities-safety-raw.md`, section 1.5.
- **[D]** our own judgement, built from the nearest source named next to it.
- **[U]** unverified: the source was weak, secondary, or not opened. Never quote it to parents as fact.
- **[P]** a proposal to test.

Important gap: the CDC milestone checklists for 4 and 5 years could not be opened [ages §A2 and §M]. This brief rests
mainly on England's Development Matters guidance (3 to 4 years and Reception, 4 to 5), the CDC parenting page for 3 to
5, Reach Out and Read, and the studies in ages §F.

---

## 1. Who this child is

**What they understand.** Sentences of four to six words, joined with "because", "or" and "and"; past and future tense;
they answer simple "why" questions [S, ages §D1, 3 to 4 years]. In the Reception year (4 to 5) they connect ideas,
describe events in detail and in order, and retell a story in their own words once they know it well [S, ages §D1].
Vocabulary figures (about 1,500 words at 4, about 2,100 at 5) come from secondary course pages only [U, ages §K].

**The big step at four.** At about age 4 children start to understand that another person can believe something that
is not true [S, ages §F9, a meta-analysis of 178 studies]. This is why our middle group starts at 4 (master plan §14).
From here a story can have a character who is mistaken, who does not know what we know, or who is surprised. Reading
questions move from "what" and "where" at 3 to "why", "how" and "what would you do?" at 4, and to "what happens next?"
at 5 [S, ages §G, Reach Out and Read].

**How they feel.** They name happy, sad, angry and worried; they slowly understand how others feel; they begin to
moderate their own feelings and to keep going when something is hard [S, ages §D1]. They want to be part of a group:
class, family, community [S, ages §D1].

**How they play.** Long pretend play with others; they find solutions to quarrels and rivalries; they follow rules,
know why, and remember them without reminders [S, ages §D1]. They can be assertive in fitting ways [S, ages §D1].
Sharing is still limited; small acts of turn-taking are realistic, fairness as an idea comes later [S, ages §F8].

**What their bodies can do.** Hop, ride a tricycle, use safety scissors, dress and undress [S, ages §B, CDC 3 to 5].
WHO advises 180 minutes of activity a day at 3 to 4, including 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous play [S, ages §C].

**Numbers and letters.** Count one item per number to 5, then beyond 10 in Reception; know that the last number
counted is the total; compare "more" and "fewer"; talk about flat and solid shapes; continue AB patterns [S, ages
§D1]. Clap syllables, hear rhymes and same first sounds, know some letters of their own name [S, ages §D1].

**What we write for.** Pitch at four and a half. The first half of the research goal list also suits a strong
three-year-old [S, ages §J2 note], and the parent decides.

## 2. Hard limits for the book

Limits are set on the English text. Arabic and Hebrew match the English in ideas per spread, not in word count,
because their words carry attached prefixes and endings [D].

| Limit | Rule | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| Total words | 200 to 450 | [D] from the single publishing source: picture books for 4 to 8 at or under 500 words (ages §G); writing guides disagree (200 to 1,000) and are weak sources [U] |
| Spreads | 12 to 14, plus a cover | [D] from the 32-page picture book (ages §G) |
| Words per spread | 15 to 40 | [D]. When this and the total disagree, the total wins: 14 spreads of 40 words would break the 450-word cap |
| Sentence length | usually 5 to 10 words; never more than 14; "because" and "but" sentences welcome | [D] from four-to-six-word sentences with "because" at about 4 (ages §D1) |
| Plot | one problem, one feeling, one turn, one warm ending; at most one surprise | [D] |
| Characters | at most 4 who speak or move the plot; one named main child who is in every spread (a whole class is never the hero) | [D] |
| Repetition | a refrain or a pattern of three (three tries, three animals, three days) | [S] AAP: text children can start to memorise and fill in (ages §G) |
| Rhyme | welcome; when used, place the word to be learned at the rhyme and leave a natural pause before it so the child can guess it | [S] the "Seuss boost" study, 3 to 5 year olds, English: rhyme that made the target word predictable helped word learning, and pausing before it helped most (ages §H; paper read only as a summary, U). Transfer to Arabic, Hebrew and Danish is U: write rhyme natively, never translate it |
| Questions to the child | one "why", "how" or "what would you do?" moment per story, built into the text or the parent note | [S] ages §G, Reach Out and Read at 4 |
| Dialogue | short lines, one speaker at a time, always tagged ("said Maya") | [D] the voice stage needs clear speakers (master plan, J3) |

**What the text may not contain.** A spoken moral at the end, sarcasm, long descriptions, more than one subplot,
flashbacks, a lesson learned through punishment or fear.

## 3. Film limits [P]

No trusted source gives attention minutes for this age; the folk rule "age plus one" must not be cited [S, ages §G].
WHO and the AAP set a daily cap for all screens of about one hour at ages 2 to 5, less is better, watched with an
adult [S, ages §C; whether the 2026 AAP policy keeps the hour is U].

| Item | Proposal to test |
|---|---|
| Length | 4 to 5 minutes [P] (master plan §3.4). The research file's own draft range was 8 to 12 minutes and the owner's first range 4 to 10; we start short and lengthen only if pilot viewers stay engaged |
| Scenes | 5 to 7, each about 40 to 60 seconds; the turn of the story in the second half [P] |
| Shot length | mostly 4 to 10 seconds; calm camera; humour through movement [P] |
| Suspense | mild, short, and resolved within the same scene [D] |
| Sound | music that follows feeling (worried, happy, calm); no sudden loud noise [D] |
| Ending | a clear end image held for a few seconds; no autoplay [S] ages §C |
| Words in picture | none; captions outside the art, in the voice's language (master plan §7) |

## 5. Tone, and what to avoid

**Tone:** warm, lively, often funny, with a real feeling at the centre. Children in the story solve the problem
themselves, with adults nearby. Adults are kind, sometimes wrong, never cruel.

**Avoid:**
- Lessons taught by punishment, fear or shame. Show the good choice and its good result [S, ages §F7: an honesty
  study of 268 children aged 3 to 7 found that a story where honesty is praised raised truth-telling, while Pinocchio
  and The Boy Who Cried Wolf did not].
- A moral spoken at the end. The child should feel the lesson, not be told it.
- Real danger: injury, being lost for long, strangers who mean harm. Safety stories end safely within a few pages [D].
- Children shown eating choking foods (whole grapes, nuts, popcorn, hard sweets) [S, safety §1.1: avoid until about
  age 4 or older, by development].
- Children shown handling chicks, reptiles, frogs or rodents [S, safety §1.11].
- Screens as the hero of a story. The research says screens should be limited and shared at this age [S, ages §C].

The owner's lessons at this age: **respect for classmates** (B02, listening while a classmate speaks); **respect for
the elderly** (B14, greeting and giving a seat); **numbers** (B01, counting to ten). **Being good to one's mother**
has no group-B story: it is in A03 and C03, and a third "help Mama" plot would repeat their shape. As noted in the research, no
trusted source names respect for elders or for classmates as a goal by itself [ages §M]; at this age we build them on
"kindness and respect for others" and "constructive and respectful relationships", which are sourced [S, ages §J2
item 10].

## 7. Activity safety checklist for group B

**Rule for the whole group:** this group starts at 4, so the under-3 rules no longer bind it, but the food rules for
"about age 4 or older, depending on development" still do [S, safety §1.1]. Every activity with food states its
allergens.

**Red list: never in a group-B activity**
- [ ] Button or coin batteries or anything containing them; magnets of any kind [S, safety §1.2; "no magnets under 6" is a derived rule, D]
- [ ] Latex balloons, whole or burst [S, safety §1.1: a choking risk up to age 8]
- [ ] Choking foods as food or as play material: whole grapes, nuts, popcorn, hard or sticky sweets, raw carrot or apple chunks, sausages; any food not cut to pieces of about 1 cm or less [S, safety §1.1]
- [ ] Small beads, necklaces, anything worn as a loop around the neck [S, safety §3 under-5 list]
- [ ] Scissors without an adult within arm's reach; any scissors other than child-size, blunt-tip ones [S, safety §1.5]
- [ ] Heat of any kind; only the adult prepares anything hot, beforehand, away from the children [S, safety §1.9 product rule]
- [ ] Knives, pins, needles, staples, craft knives, split pins [S, safety §3 and §2]
- [ ] Water deeper than a few centimetres, or water play without an adult watching the whole time; empty the tray after [S, safety §1.7]
- [ ] Berries, seeds for tasting, mushrooms, bulbs, unknown flowers; picking plants from garden beds. In Israel: arum, oleander, castor bean. In Denmark: yew, laburnum [S, safety §1.3 and §1.11]
- [ ] Household chemicals, medicines, permanent markers, solvent glue, spray paint, art products without the AP seal [S, safety §1.6 and §3]
- [ ] Dyes or inks that stain the mouth [S, safety §3]
- [ ] Chicks, reptiles, frogs, rodents [S, safety §1.11]
- [ ] Plastic bags; string tied to anything a child can pull around a body part [S, safety §3; D for the string rule]
- [ ] Raw flour and raw-flour dough, including craft and play dough [S, CDC "Raw Flour and Dough": untreated flour can carry E. coli; checked in the review, outside our research files]

**Safe materials for group B** (everything in the group-A list, plus) [D, safety §2]
Blunt child scissors with thick paper, washable glue stick or white glue with the AP seal, cardboard offcuts,
yoghurt pots, paper cups, egg cartons (rinsed; check egg allergy), leaves, sticks and pebbles the size of a fist or
bigger collected with an adult, dough only when the adult has cooked it or heat-treated the flour beforehand (never
raw-flour dough: CDC "Raw Flour and Dough"; wheat allergy note; not for eating), washable markers,
chalk, coloured tape, sponges, spoons and funnels, cotton wool, string no longer than a hand span tied only by the
adult, clothes pegs.

**Allergy check before starting** [D, safety §1.10; no trusted source on allergens in craft was found, U]
Wheat, egg, milk, nuts (never used), sesame (never used in food activities; added by us because it is common in
Israeli foods such as tahini and za'atar, D), latex (never used).

**Outdoors** [S, safety §1.11] Collect only leaves, sticks, stones bigger than a toilet-paper tube opening, large
pinecones and dry grass; "we never put plants in our mouths"; hands washed after. In hot months collect before 10:00
or after 16:00 [D, safety §1.11, the researcher's own rule]; a hat and shade always [S, safety §1.11].

**Emergency line on every activity card:** Israel 101 and Denmark 112 [U, from general knowledge; the Israeli poison
centre number was not confirmed, safety §1.12].
