# Style guide: English for StoryTime read-aloud text

For writers and reviewers of StoryTime's English stories. Tags: [S] sourced (file and section named), [D] our
judgement, [U] unverified, [P] proposal to test. Research file: `ages` = `docs/research/ages-raw.md`.

English is also the working language of the Story Room: outlines and briefs are written in English first. That does
not make the English text the "original". Each language is written natively from the shared outline; English must
not become a template the others translate word for word (master plan §10, Stage 6).

---

## 1. Register and spelling

1. Plain, warm, concrete English; the words a good parent would use with a child. Nouns and verbs the child can see
   in the picture [D]. Children learn nouns more easily than verbs from shared reading [S, ages §H, Seuss-boost study,
   summary only, U].
2. **British spelling** (colour, favourite, mum) as the default [D; an open question in the README: Israeli families
   may know American English better].
3. Parents and grandparents in dialogue and group-A narration: Mum, Dad, Grandma, Grandpa. In group B and C narration
   "her mum", "his grandfather" are fine.
4. Narration in the past tense; group A may use the present tense; one tense per book.
5. Grammar to avoid in group A: the passive, relative clauses, "if" sentences, phrasal verbs that need a second
   meaning ("put up with"). Rare in group B; free in group C [D].

---

## 2. Sentence rhythm for reading aloud

1. One idea per sentence. Group A: 2 to 6 words; group B: 5 to 10; group C: 8 to 14, varied [D, from the age briefs].
2. The strongest word lands at the end of the sentence and at the end of the spread, where the voice falls and the
   page turns.
3. Refrains repeat word for word, so the child can join in [S, ages §G: repetitive text helps toddlers predict what
   comes next; children finish familiar phrases when the adult pauses].
4. Rhyme only when it is natural. A forced rhyme or a twisted word order ("so happy was she") is never worth it. When
   a rhyme teaches a new word, put the word at the rhyme and let the adult pause before it [S, ages §H, Seuss boost,
   3 to 5 year olds, summary only].
5. Sound words (splash, hush, tap-tap) are welcome; they give the voice something to play [D].
6. Use punctuation for pauses: the newest voice model has no break tags and reads ellipses and punctuation as pauses
   [S, docs/research/vowel-marks-raw.md §4.2]. Use … sparingly.
7. Read every sentence aloud before saving it.

---

## 3. Speaking to parents and teachers without assuming gender

1. "You" is neutral; use it freely. Never "mum" or "dad" as the default reader: "the grown-up", "the adult", "parent
   or teacher" [D; project rule 5].
2. Third person: "they" for an unknown adult ("Ask the grown-up to fold the paper; they keep the scissors").
3. Activity steps: short imperatives ("Put one stone in each cup.").
4. Do not write "boys and girls"; write "children" or "everyone".

---

## 4. Names

1. One name per character across all four languages, spelled as in the name ledger at the end of `outlines.md` [D].
2. **Keep** the name; **adapt** only the spelling where needed; **translate** names that are ordinary words (a cat
   called "Cloud"); **change** a name only if it means something rude or silly in one language, and record it.
3. Names shared by all the Abrahamic traditions (Adam, Yousef, Elias) are welcome; names tied to only one community's
   religious figures, public figures, or the owner's family (for example Sari) are not used [D].
4. Write names so an English-speaking adult reads them correctly at first sight; where a name could be misread, add
   its sound once in the parent note, not in the story.

---

## 5. Numerals

1. In story text, numbers are words ("three boats"), which helps the voice and teaches number words [D].
2. Digits never appear in story text or inside any picture or film, in any group (master plan §7): clock times are
   words ("half past three"); clock faces carry twelve marks; charts carry ticks; countdowns are shrinking dots.
   Digits appear only in adult-facing activity text ("10 to 20 cups").

---

## 6. Punctuation and typesetting

1. Double quotation marks for dialogue (“...”), curly, not straight. The speaker is named in a tag ("said Lina") when
   it is not obvious [D; the voice stage must know who speaks].
2. One space after a full stop. No semicolons in groups A and B. Exclamation marks: at most one exclamation mark per spread in groups B and C. In group A, sound words and short calls may each carry
   one (Splash! Found you!), never more than one per sentence [D].
3. The em dash only in group C; use a full stop instead where possible.
4. No hyphenation across lines in the book.

---

## 7. Words and content to avoid

- Idioms a child takes literally ("it's raining cats and dogs") unless the story plays with them on purpose.
- Labels that shame: naughty, lazy, silly (about a child), crybaby; teasing about bodies, skin, hair, accent [D].
- Gender rules as morals ("big boys don't cry") [D].
- Religious formulas and holidays; political words; army and police; real place names [D].
- Brand names, products, screens as rewards [D].
- Food that excludes families: pork, bacon, ham; wine; meat together with milk or cheese in one meal [D].
- Praise for being "smart" or "clever"; praise effort instead ("you worked hard") [S, ages §B, CDC 6 to 8].
- Americanisms and Britishisms mixed in one story.

---

## 8. Cultural neutrality for every family in Israel

English readers in Israel may be Jewish, Arab, or from any other background; some families also read in Danish.

1. No holidays, sabbath or religious symbols as the subject or decoration of a story [D].
2. Places are types: the sea, the market, the desert, the hills, a city, a village. No real place names, maps, flags,
   or army and police uniforms; a lifeguard, a bus driver, a nurse are fine [D].
3. Shared foods: bread, pita, olives, oranges, watermelon, pomegranates, cucumbers, tomatoes, dates, figs, rice.
4. Families of many shapes: mother and father, a single parent, grandparents raising a child, big and small families
   [S, ages §J2 item 34; the limits are D].
5. Clothes everyday and modest enough for any family; no pigs; dogs outdoors, not licking faces [D].
6. Days of the week that mark a sabbath (Friday, Saturday, Sunday) are not named; use "one morning", "on a holiday
   from kindergarten" [D].
7. The local year: winter rain, spring anemones, summer sea and heat, autumn first rain and migrating birds.

## 9. Reviewer's checklist (tick all ten)

- [ ] 1. Plain, concrete, warm English written from the outline, not a word-for-word source for the other languages.
- [ ] 2. British spelling throughout; one tense per book.
- [ ] 3. Sentence lengths and word count fit the age brief.
- [ ] 4. Refrains repeat word for word; rhymes are natural, with no twisted word order.
- [ ] 5. Every line of dialogue has a clear speaker.
- [ ] 6. Adults are "the grown-up", "parent or teacher", "they"; no assumed mum or dad.
- [ ] 7. Names match the ledger exactly.
- [ ] 8. Numbers are words in every group; no digits in the story text.
- [ ] 9. Punctuation follows section 6.
- [ ] 10. Nothing in sections 7 and 8 is broken: no shaming label, no "smart" praise, no religion or politics, no excluded food.
