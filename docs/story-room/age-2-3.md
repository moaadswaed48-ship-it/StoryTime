# Working brief: ages 2 to 3 (group A, stories A01 to A15)

This brief stands alone. A writer, an artist or an activity designer working on a group-A story needs only this page
plus the style guide for their language. A three-year-old belongs to this group until the fourth birthday.

**How to read the tags.** Every rule carries one tag:
- **[S]** a source states it. The reference after it points into the research files: `ages §K` means
  `docs/research/ages-raw.md`, section K; `safety §1.5` means `docs/research/activities-safety-raw.md`, section 1.5.
- **[D]** our own judgement, built from the nearest source named next to it. Reasonable, but not a finding.
- **[U]** unverified: the source was weak, secondary, or not opened. Never quote it to parents as fact.
- **[P]** a proposal to test. It changes when the pilot shows something better.

---

## 1. Who this child is

**What they understand.** Around age 2 a child has about 50 words and starts joining two words ("more milk"); by age 3
about 300 words and phrases of up to five words [S, ages §D1 and §K]. Vocabulary varies a lot between children of the
same age, so treat these as averages [S, ages §F10]. They understand more than they say: a three-key-word instruction
such as "Can you wash dolly's face?" works [S, ages §D1]. They follow a simple story "with the help of the pictures" [S,
ages §D1]. They point to things in a book when asked ("Where is the bear?") and can name actions in a picture
("running", "eating") by age 3 [S, ages §A]. They ask who, what, where and why by age 3 [S, ages §A].

**What they cannot do yet.** They do not yet understand that someone else can believe something that is wrong; that
step arrives at about age 4 [S, ages §F9 and §I]. So a group-A story must never depend on a character being fooled,
mistaken, hiding the truth or telling a lie. Sharing is still mostly self-serving at this age; real fairness comes
years later [S, ages §F8]. Abstract time words (yesterday, next week) are beyond them; "now" and "later" are in
reach [S, ages §D1 words for time].

**How they feel.** This is the age of "me do it" and of tantrums [S, ages §D1]. They are starting to put feelings into
words ("I'm sad") [S, ages §D1]. By 2 they notice when someone is hurt or upset and may pause or look sad [S, ages §A].
By 3 most calm within about ten minutes after a parent leaves them at childcare [S, ages §A]. They look at a trusted
face to know how to react to something new [S, ages §A]. Development Matters says children at this age can "safely
explore emotions beyond their normal range through play and stories" [S, ages §D1].

**What their bodies can do.** Run, kick a ball, climb a few stairs, eat with a spoon at 2; string large beads, pull
on loose clothes, use a fork, draw a circle when shown at 3 [S, ages §A].

**How they listen.** They choose their own activity and are hard to direct; they are easily distracted; saying their
name helps them switch [S, ages §D1]. They love the same book for weeks, and between 24 and 36 months they "read"
along with text they know by heart [S, ages §G, KidsHealth, a hospital source]. It is fine if they do not sit through
a whole book [S, ages §G]. A child who walks around during reading is still listening [S, ages §G, Reach Out and
Read].

**What we write for.** Pitch every story at a child of about two and a half. A child just turned three will still
enjoy it; a child near four may be ready for group B, and the parent chooses [D, ages §I recommendation 2].

## 2. Hard limits for the book

These limits are written for the English text. Arabic and Hebrew pack more meaning into one written word (prefixes and
endings attach to the word), so their word counts come out lower; they must match the English in the number of ideas
per spread, not in the number of words [D].

| Limit | Rule | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| Total words | 40 to 150 | [D] from the single publishing source: board books for 0 to 3 under 50 words, for 2 to 5 under 200 words (ages §G; the source itself is one professional article, so treat as a range, not a rule) |
| Spreads (page openings) | 8 to 12, plus a cover | [D] from "8 to 24 pages" for board books (ages §G) |
| Words per spread | 3 to 15 | [D]. When this and the total disagree, the total wins: 12 spreads of 15 words would break the 150-word cap |
| Sentence length | usually 2 to 6 words; never more than 8 | [D] from about 300 words and up-to-five-word phrases at age 3 (ages §D1) |
| Ideas per spread | one action or one discovery | [D] from "understand what is happening with the help of the pictures" (ages §D1) |
| Characters | at most 3 characters who speak or move the plot, counting a toy or animal that acts; at most 2 of them children. Background figures (a bird flying past, a seller behind a stall) may appear but never change what happens | [D] |
| Repetition | a refrain the child can predict and join, on at least two of every three spreads; the same sentence frame each time ("One! Two! Three! Wheee!") | [S] AAP: "repetitive phrases or rhymes that can help toddlers predict what comes next" (ages §G); KidsHealth: pause so the child finishes familiar phrases (ages §G) |
| Rhyme | welcome, never forced; a rhyming refrain is better than a rhymed whole text. Write rhyme natively in each language; never translate a rhyme | [S] AAP ages §G for rhyme; the rhyme-and-word-learning study was on 3 to 5 year olds in English only, so its transfer is [U] (ages §H) |
| Structure | a familiar routine, one small problem, a warm return to safety | [D] |
| Questions to the child | at most one "Where is...?" or "What is...?" per spread, answered by the picture | [S] what and where questions at 24 to 30 months (ages §G, Reach Out and Read) |
| Picture role | the picture carries the story; the text names what the child sees | [S] ages §D1 |

**What the text may not contain.** Metaphors ("flour is snow"); a comparison with "like" only when both things can be
seen or heard in the same picture ("round, like the wheel"); irony, sarcasm, "yesterday" and "tomorrow" as plot devices, flashbacks,
two things happening at once, a narrator's moral at the end.

## 3. Film limits [P]

No trusted source gives "minutes of attention" by age. The popular "age plus one minute" rule is folk wisdom and must
not be cited [S, ages §G]. WHO and the AAP give a daily cap for all screens together: no more than one hour a day for
ages 2 to 4, less is better, watched with an adult [S, ages §C; whether the 2026 AAP policy keeps the one-hour figure
is U]. So our film is a small part of that hour.

| Item | Proposal to test |
|---|---|
| Length | 2 to 3 minutes [P] (master plan §3.4; the research file's own draft suggested segments of 3 to 6 minutes, ages §G) |
| Scenes | 3 or 4, each built on the same repeated beat as the book's refrain [P] |
| Shot length | mostly 6 to 12 seconds; slow camera moves; one thing moves at a time [P]. Background: one secondary study found preschoolers look at the screen in bursts averaging about 13 seconds and look longer when the content makes sense to them [U, ages §G] |
| Sound | soft music, clear natural sounds, no sudden loud noise, no scary music [D] |
| Ending | a calm closing image that holds for several seconds, then stops. No autoplay, no "next film" [S] AAP warns against autoplay (ages §C) |
| Adult | the page around the film invites the adult to watch together [S] co-viewing, ages §C |
| Words in picture | none; captions sit outside the art in the voice's language (master plan §7 and H4) |

## 4. Activity limits

| Item | Rule | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| Length | 5 to 10 minutes of core activity | [P] safety §4: the only peer-reviewed figure is 2 to 8 minutes of sustained attention in preschoolers (via a search summary); the per-age minutes come from low-quality blogs |
| Steps | one main action, repeated (the child does the same thing several times) | [P] safety §4 |
| Instructions | one spoken instruction at a time; two key words are fine | [S] two-step directions about 2.5 to 3 years is from secondary sources (safety §4, so [U] in detail); three-key-word understanding (ages §D1) |
| Adult role | hands-on partner: does it first, does it with the child, names every object and action aloud | [D] safety §4 synthesis of NAEYC themes |
| Group version | one adult with one child, or one adult with at most 4 children. For anything with water, food or paint: one adult per 4 children, all within arm's reach | [D] safety §1.4 and §4 (NAEYC care ratios of about 1:9 are for care, not for hands-on activities; the 1:6 craft ratio in the research is the researcher's own rule, so we tighten it further for this group) |
| Allergy | ask about allergies before any food, dough or plant material | [D] safety §1.10 |
| End | wash hands after every activity with soil, plants, paint, dough or animals | [S] safety §1.11 |

## 5. Tone, and what to avoid

**Tone:** calm, warm, close. Funny in a physical way (a hat falls off, a camel sneezes). The grown-ups are kind and
present. The world is safe. Small worries (Papa is stuck on the slide, thunder is loud, Papa has gone to work) are always solved
inside the story.

**Avoid:**
- Anything scary: monsters, villains, being lost alone, darkness as danger, angry adults, injury shown [D].
- Stories that need the child to understand a lie, a trick, a surprise kept secret or a mistaken belief [S, ages §F9].
- Shaming, threats or punishment as the lesson. Show the good behaviour and its good result [S, ages §F7: the honesty
  study found that showing the reward of good behaviour works and showing punishment does not; that study was on ages
  3 to 7].
- A moral spoken by a grown-up at the end.
- Jealousy, embarrassment and other complex feelings as the main subject [D, craft advice from the kids-book-writer
  skill, unsourced].
- Small details the child must find to follow the plot [D].
- Foods that are choking risks shown being eaten by the child (whole grapes, nuts, popcorn, hard sweets) [S, safety
  §1.1]. A picture teaches as much as a sentence.
- A child shown holding chicks, reptiles or rodents [S, safety §1.11: unsuitable around children under 5].

The owner's lessons at this age: **respect for the elderly** (as walking at an older person's pace, story A04);
**being good to one's mother** (as thanking her and giving back with a small help, story A03); **respect for
classmates** (as taking turns with a classmate, story A15); **numbers** (counting to three, story A01). There is no trusted source for "respect for elders" as a goal at any age (ages §J3 item 15 and
§M); at this age we build it on effortful control and noticing others, which are sourced [D].

## 7. Activity safety checklist for group A

**The rule for the whole group:** because the group includes two-year-olds, every group-A activity follows the
under-3 rules, even when the child in front of the adult is three and a half [D, safety §1.1].

**The tube test.** Anything that fits through an empty toilet-paper tube is a choking hazard and stays out [S, safety
§1.1; the tube's diameter of about 4.4 cm is U]. This is a home stand-in for the US small-parts rule for products for
children under 3 [S, safety §1.1].

**Red list: never in a group-A activity**
- [ ] Button or coin batteries, or anything that contains them; magnets of any kind [S, safety §1.2; "no magnets under 6" is the researcher's derived rule, D]
- [ ] Anything smaller than the tube test: beads, buttons, googly eyes, sequins, glitter, pom-poms, coins, marbles, bottle caps, pen caps, small stones [S, safety §1.1 and §3]
- [ ] Latex balloons, whole or burst [S, safety §1.1: a choking risk up to age 8]
- [ ] Choking foods: whole grapes, nuts and seeds, popcorn, hard or sticky sweets, raw carrot or apple chunks, sausages, whole beans, dry rice, lentils or pasta as play material [S, safety §1.1 and §1.10]
- [ ] Scissors in the child's hand (the adult does all cutting; the child tears) [S, safety §1.5; this is a developmental range, not a law]
- [ ] String, ribbon, yarn or cloth strips longer than a hand span (about 15 cm); anything that makes a loop or goes around the neck [D, safety §1.8; length limits were not verified]
- [ ] Water deeper than a few centimetres, or any water without an adult's hand within reach; empty the tray straight after [S, safety §1.7]
- [ ] Heat of any kind: stove, oven, kettle, candles, hot glue [S, safety §1.9 and §3]
- [ ] Knives, pins, needles, staples, craft knives [S, safety §3]
- [ ] Berries, seeds, mushrooms, bulbs, unknown flowers; any plant tasted or crushed. In Israel: arum, oleander, castor bean. In Denmark: yew, laburnum [S, safety §1.3 and §1.11]
- [ ] Household chemicals, medicines, detergent pods, permanent markers, solvent glue, spray paint, art products without the AP seal [S, safety §1.6 and §3]
- [ ] Cotton wool, tissues or paper small enough to be pulled apart and put in the mouth [S, safety §3]
- [ ] Chicks, reptiles, frogs, rodents [S, safety §1.11]
- [ ] Plastic bags [S, safety §3]
- [ ] Raw flour and raw-flour dough, including craft and play dough: the CDC advises that children should not play with or eat it, because untreated flour can carry E. coli [S, CDC "Raw Flour and Dough"; this source is outside our research files and was checked in the review]
- [ ] Cardboard boxes with staples left in: take staples out and tape the edges [S, safety §3 for staples]

**Safe materials for group A** (all pass the tube test) [D, safety §2]
Large cardboard boxes, kitchen-roll tubes, paper plates, paper cups (large), paper bags, newspaper for tearing,
washed cloth scraps without loops, washed socks, plastic tubs (without small lids), wooden spoons, pots and pans,
chunky crayons, washable AP-seal paint and finger paint, water in a shallow tray, large leaves from a plant the adult
knows (never oleander, arum or castor bean, safety §1.11), large pinecones,
smooth stones bigger than the child's fist, sand or soil (hands washed after), short scarves, a soft toy, cushions,
a blanket.

**Allergy check before starting** [D, safety §1.10; no trusted source on allergens in craft was found, U]
Wheat (bread; flour is never used raw), egg (egg cartons), milk (some paints and glues), nuts (never use), latex (never use).

**Emergency line on every activity card:** the local emergency number. Israel 101 and Denmark 112 are from general
knowledge; the Israeli poison centre number was not confirmed [U, safety §1.12]. Check both before publishing.
