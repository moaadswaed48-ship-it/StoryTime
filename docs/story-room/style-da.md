# Style guide: Danish (dansk) for StoryTime read-aloud text

For writers and reviewers of StoryTime's Danish stories. Tags: [S] sourced (file and section named), [D] our
judgement, [U] unverified, [P] proposal to test. Research file: `ages` = `docs/research/ages-raw.md`.

**What the research could not give us:** no authoritative source was found on Danish age-band conventions for
children's books, and the Danish rhyme tradition (remser) could not be sourced [S that both are missing, ages §H].
Denmark's curriculum sets goals for the learning environment, not for the individual child, and puts play,
"dannelse" and children's communities first [S, ages §D2, BUPL leaflet]. So Danish stories should invite play and
togetherness, not drill. A native Danish reader matters most in this language (master plan §12).

**Write Danish first, not as a translation** of the English (master plan §10, Stage 6).

---

## 1. Register

1. Everyday standard Danish (rigsdansk), the way a calm parent or pædagog talks to a child. No regional slang, no
   bookish words where an everyday word exists [D].
2. No English loanwords where a Danish word exists (cool, okay, nice) [D].
3. Parents in dialogue and group-A narration: mor, far. Grandparents: bedstemor and bedstefar in narration; in
   dialogue the writer may use mormor, farmor, morfar or farfar only once the outline fixes which side of the family
   the grandparent is on, and then keeps it [D].
4. Narration in the past tense; group A may use the present tense; one tense per book.
5. Grammar to avoid in group A: the passive (s-passive and blive-passive), long subordinate clauses, conditionals.
   Rare in group B; free in group C [D].

---

## 2. Sentence rhythm for reading aloud

1. One idea per sentence. Group A: 2 to 6 words; group B: 5 to 10; group C: 8 to 14, varied [D, from the age briefs].
   Danish compounds count as one word; avoid long compounds in group A (not "badeværelsesvasken") [D].
2. The important word comes at the end of the sentence; Danish main clauses are verb-second, so start with the scene
   ("Ved havet stod Adam.") when you want the subject to land later [D].
3. Refrains repeat word for word [S, ages §G].
4. Rhyme only when natural, written in Danish from the start; never translate a rhyme [S that rhyme transfer is
   unverified, ages §H]. Danish read-aloud tradition loves short remser and sound play; use them in refrains [U, the
   tradition itself could not be sourced].
5. Sound words (plask, bum, tik-tak) are welcome.
6. Pauses come from punctuation; the newest voice model has no break tags [S, docs/research/vowel-marks-raw.md §4.2].
7. Read every sentence aloud before saving it.

---

## 3. Speaking to parents and teachers without assuming gender

Danish "du" is neutral and is the normal way to address any adult.

1. Steps and captions: short imperatives ("Læg én sten i hver kop.") [D].
2. To the adult and child together: "I" ("Læs historien sammen, I to.") [D].
3. Never "mor" as the default reader: "den voksne", "forælder eller pædagog" (pædagog is gender-neutral and is the
   Danish word for a kindergarten teacher) [D].
4. Third person for an unknown adult: "den voksne" and "vedkommende", or rewrite the sentence without a pronoun.

---

## 4. Names

1. One name per character across all four languages, spelled as in the name ledger at the end of `outlines.md` [D].
2. **Adapt** the Latin spelling where a Danish reader would misread it: Danish reads Y as a vowel, so Yasmin becomes
   Jasmin, Yousef becomes Josef, Maya becomes Maja, Yara becomes Jara. The sound stays the same [D].
3. **Translate** names that are ordinary words (a cat called "Cloud" becomes Sky); **change** a name only if it is
   rude or silly in Danish, and record it in the ledger.
4. Names shared by all the Abrahamic traditions (Adam, Josef, Elias) are welcome; names tied to only one community's
   religious figures, public figures, or the owner's family (for example Sari) are not used [D].

---

## 5. Numerals

1. In story text, numbers are words ("tre både"). Note the Danish tens from fifty upward (halvtreds, tres, halvfjerds,
   firs, halvfems): in group C maths stories above twenty, write digits and check how the voice reads them [D].
2. Clock times in group C: words in the text ("klokken fire", "halv fem"). Warn the writer: Danish "halv fem" means
   4:30, which differs from English "half five" in some regions; the picture must show the same time as the text [D].
3. Films carry no digits or words inside the picture (master plan §7).

---

## 6. Punctuation and typesetting

1. Dialogue in »guillemets pointing inward«, with the speaker named when needed ("sagde Lina") [D].
2. Commas follow Dansk Sprognævn's current rules; we write without the optional start comma [U: confirm the current
   rule with the editor].
3. No semicolons in groups A and B; at most one exclamation mark per spread.
4. No hyphenation across lines in the book.

---

## 7. Words and content to avoid

- Slang and English loanwords (section 1).
- Labels that shame: fræk, doven, dum (about a child), tudeprins; teasing about bodies, skin, hair, accent [D].
- Gender rules as morals ("store drenge græder ikke") [D].
- Danish holidays and church festivals (jul, påske, fastelavn) and any other holiday as the subject [D].
- Brand names, products, screens as rewards [D].
- Food that excludes families in Israel: pork (flæskesteg, pølser of pork, leverpostej), wine, meat with milk or
  cheese in one meal (no "frikadeller med flødesovs" scenes) [D].
- Praise for being "klog"; praise effort ("du kæmpede virkelig") [S, ages §B, CDC 6 to 8].

---

## 8. Cultural neutrality for every family in Israel

The Danish text is read by Danish-speaking families in Israel and by families in Denmark. Stories happen in a shared
picture world built for Israel's landscapes; the Danish text does not explain or relocate them [D].

1. No holidays, sabbath or religious symbols as subject or decoration [D].
2. Places are types: the sea, the market, the desert, the hills, a city, a village. No real place names, maps, flags,
   or army and police uniforms [D].
3. Shared foods: bread, pita, olives, oranges, watermelon, pomegranates, cucumbers, tomatoes, dates, figs, rice.
4. Families of many shapes [S, ages §J2 item 34; the limits are D].
5. Clothes everyday and modest enough for any family; no pigs; dogs outdoors, not licking faces [D].
6. The seasons are those of the eastern Mediterranean: winter means rain, rarely snow in the hills; summer means heat.
   Do not add Danish snow or Danish summer light to a story [D].
7. Weekdays that mark a sabbath (fredag, lørdag, søndag) are not named [D].

- [ ] 2. No slang, no English loanwords, no long compounds in group A.
- [ ] 3. Sentence lengths and word count fit the age brief; one tense per book.
- [ ] 4. Refrains repeat word for word; rhymes are natural Danish.
- [ ] 5. Every line of dialogue has a clear speaker; »guillemets« used.
- [ ] 6. Adults are addressed with du, I, "den voksne" or "forælder eller pædagog"; no assumed mother or father.
- [ ] 7. Names match the ledger, with the Danish spelling adaptations (J for Y).
- [ ] 8. Numbers are words; group-C digits only where the digits are the lesson; clock times match the pictures.
- [ ] 9. Punctuation follows section 6.
