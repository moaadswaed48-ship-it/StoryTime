### Activity

- **title** One Stone, One Cup
- **goal** Put one thing in each place, and say one number for each thing.
- **materials[0]** Three paper or plastic cups
- **materials[1]** Three smooth, washed stones, each bigger than the child’s fist
- **materials[2]** A box or basket for the stones
- **steps[0].caption** Three cups. Three stones.
- **steps[1].caption** One stone in each cup. Count!
- **steps[2].caption** Tip them out. Again!
- **steps[3].caption** Into the box: one, two, three.
- **safety** Only stones bigger than the child’s fist, smooth and washed. Play sitting on the floor: drop the stones, never throw them. Nothing goes in the mouth. If there is an accident, call your local emergency number.
- **forAdult** In the story, Adam says one number for each step and each pigeon. In this game the hands do the same thing: one stone, one cup, one number. Saying one number for each thing is one of the building blocks of counting. Set three cups in a row. The stones must be bigger than the child’s fist, washed and dry: anything that fits through a toilet-paper tube is too small. Start yourself: drop the first stone into a cup and say “One!”, then it is the child’s turn. Count slowly, one number as each stone drops, and hold up one more finger each time. Then tip the cups over and start again. To make it easier, use one cup and one stone. To make it harder, use four cups and four stones. Five to ten minutes is plenty, and it is best to stop while it is still fun.
- **inClass** One adult with up to four children, sitting in a circle on the floor. Each child has three cups and three stones. The adult counts slowly and clearly, and the children drop a stone on each number. Then everyone tips their cups over together and says “Again!”

### Activity

- **title** The Looking Frame
- **goal** Look closely at small things in nature and learn their names.
- **materials[0]** One paper plate
- **steps[0].caption** The grown-up tears out the middle.
- **steps[1].caption** Hold hands. Walk slowly.
- **steps[2].caption** Stop. Hold up the frame.
- **steps[3].caption** What’s inside? Say its name.
- **steps[4].caption** Back home. Wash hands.
- **safety** Look only: no picking, no touching insects, nothing in the mouth. The grown-up tears the plate and throws the middle away. If there is an accident, call your local emergency number.
- **forAdult** In the story, Sami only sees the butterfly, the ladybird and the bee once he slows down to Grandpa’s pace. This walk lets the child feel the same thing: when we go slowly, there is more to see. The paper frame helps a two- or three-year-old look at one small thing at a time, and gives you both a moment to name it. Hearing the name of the thing they are looking at helps toddlers learn new words. Tear or cut the middle out of a paper plate yourself (scissors stay in adult hands) and put the middle in the bin, because small pieces of paper can end up in a mouth. Walk outside slowly, hand in hand, at the pace of the slowest walker. Every few steps, stop and say “Stop and look!” The child holds the frame up to something (a flower, a stone, an ant, a cloud), and you say its name together, adding one word: “a red flower”, “a tiny ant”, “a big cloud”. Let the child choose what to frame. Framing the same thing again and again is fine. What to watch: we look, but we do not pick or touch, especially insects and plants. Some plants are poisonous (in Israel, oleander, arum and castor bean; in Denmark, yew and laburnum). Keep a sun hat on. Five to ten minutes is plenty. To make it easier, frame one thing yourself and ask “What is that?” To make it harder, ask “Can you find something red?” or “Can you find something small?” Wash hands when you come home.
- **inClass** One adult walks with up to four children: two pairs, each pair sharing one frame. The adult walks slowly in front and calls “Stop and look!” every few steps. One pair holds up its frame and names one thing. The other pair says the word back. Then the pairs swap. Back inside, everyone washes hands.

- **materials[2]** A mirror

### Activity

- **title** Blow the Paper Ball
- **goal** Practise long, slow breaths out through play.
- **materials[0]** One whole sheet of newspaper
- **steps[0].caption** Crumple one big paper ball.
- **steps[1].caption** Lie down, face to face.
- **steps[2].caption** Blow slowly: whoooo!
- **steps[3].caption** Now blow it back.
- **steps[4].caption** Afterwards, wash hands.
- **safety** One whole sheet crumpled into one big ball: no small torn pieces, and nothing in the mouth. A few slow breaths, then a rest. Stop if the child feels dizzy. Wash hands afterwards (newspaper ink). If there is an accident, call your local emergency number.
- **forAdult** In the story, Grandma helps Yara calm down by breathing slowly with her: in… out… This game practises the same long, slow breath out, through play. Grown-ups use slow breaths to calm down, and a rolling ball turns a breath into something a toddler can see. Crumple a whole sheet of newspaper into one big ball together. The child can help squeeze it. Lie on your tummies, face to face, about an arm’s length apart. Breathe in, then blow out slowly and for a long time, “whoooo”, so the ball rolls to the child. Then the child blows it back. Praise the long, slow breath, not the speed. To make it easier, lie closer together. To make it harder, stand two books up as a gate and blow the ball through it. A few breaths at a time, with rests in between. Five to ten minutes in all is plenty. Wash hands afterwards, because newspaper ink comes off on fingers. The game also works at bedtime, or on a stormy night.
- **inClass** Pairs lie face to face on a mat, one paper ball per pair, with one adult for every four children. The adult leads one slow “whoooo” for everyone, and all the balls roll at once. Then the other child in each pair blows. Everyone washes hands afterwards.

- **goal** See that taking one away leaves one less: ten, then nine.

- **materials[1]** Ten large pinecones, or ten stones bigger than a fist

### Activity

- **title** The Talking Stick
- **goal** Listen to a friend and say back what they said.
- **materials[0]** A short, smooth stick from the garden, or a wooden spoon
- **steps[0].caption** Only the one holding the stick talks.
- **steps[1].caption** Pass it on. Say back one thing you heard.
- **steps[2].caption** Now say your own thing.

- **forAdult** In the story, Dalia is only heard when everyone stops and listens. This game practises that, and goes one step further: showing someone you really heard them by saying back what they said. Listening like this is a way of showing respect to the person who is speaking, and children get better at it with practice. Sit in a circle on the floor (two people are enough; three or four is better). Choose an easy topic for the first round, such as “an animal I like” or “something that made me laugh today”. Whoever holds the stick talks. Everyone else listens with closed mouths and still hands. Then the stick passes on. Before the next person says their own thing, they say back one thing they heard: “You like cats, because they are soft.” Go first yourself, so the children hear how it sounds, and help with the first words: “You said…” Praise the listening, not only the talking: “You remembered exactly what Grandma said!” Ten to twenty minutes, or two or three rounds, is plenty. To make it easier, the listener says back only the speaker’s name and one word. To make it harder, say back two things, or ask the speaker one question about what they said before taking your own turn. If a child forgets, the speaker simply says it again: forgetting is part of learning.
- **inClass** Circles of six children, each circle with one adult and one stick. The adult speaks first, then passes the stick. When the first child has spoken, the adult says back what that child said, so everyone hears how it is done. Then the game goes on round the circle. Keep the same topic for the whole round. Notice good listening out loud: “You heard every word.”

### Activity

- **title** How Big Is My Feeling?
- **goal** Show how big a feeling is on a ladder of three steps.
- **materials[0]** A long strip of paper or card
- **materials[1]** A crayon
- **materials[2]** A wooden clothes peg
- **steps[0].caption** Draw three steps: small, middle, big.

- **inClass** Draw one big ladder of three steps on a long strip of paper and hang it on the wall, with a big peg or a paper arrow. The teacher gives a “what if”, and one child moves the peg and says why. The others show with their hands whether their own feeling would be smaller, the same, or bigger. Children take turns at the ladder.

### Activity

- **title** Clap the Rope Rhyme
- **goal** Keep one shared rhythm as a group.
- **materials[0]** A long rope (about four or five metres)
- **materials[1]** A flat, open space outdoors or in a hall
- **steps[0].caption** Clap a rhyme together.
- **steps[1].caption** Two turners: slow and low.
- **steps[2].caption** Jump when the rope touches the ground.
- **steps[3].caption** Guess first: is fast or slow harder?
- **steps[4].caption** Swap turners. Everyone turns once.
- **safety** Turn the rope low and slowly, on flat ground, with shoes on. Hold the ends in your hands; never wrap the rope round a hand, an arm or a neck. When nobody is turning it, the rope lies flat on the ground. One jumper at a time. If there is an accident, call your local emergency number.
- **forAdult** In the story, Mira notices a classmate sitting alone and invites him to turn the rope with her. Slow turns out to be just right for many children, and the game ends up with a place for everyone. This activity lets children feel the same thing: a group that keeps one rhythm together makes room for every kind of jumper. Start without the rope. Then two children take the ends of the rope and turn it slowly, low, so that it touches the ground on every clap. The others clap the beat while one child at a time jumps in. Many children are surprised that slow needs more waiting and more listening. Swap the turners every round, so that everyone turns at least once; turning is a real skill, not a waiting job. Praise the effort and the listening (“you waited for the rope”), not who jumps most. Twenty minutes is about right. To make it easier, clap and step over a rope lying still on the ground. To make it harder, try a fast round, then a slow one, and see whether the group can change speed together.
- **inClass** Groups of six, each with its own long rope and space. Two turn, one leads the clapping and three jump in, one at a time; then everyone moves one place along, so that each child turns once. The teacher listens from group to group and asks, “Is everyone clapping at the same moment?” Finish with the whole class clapping the beat together.

## C06 · One Note at a Time

### Book

- **p1** It was winter, and the school hall smelled of rain and floor polish. “The school concert is in thirty days,” said the music teacher. “Who would like to play something?” Lily’s hand shot up before she had even decided. Then she knew exactly what she wanted to play.

### Activity

- **title** Bottle Music
- **goal** Discover how the amount of water changes a note’s pitch.
- **materials[0]** Four empty plastic bottles of the same size, caps off
- **materials[1]** Water and a jug
- **materials[2]** A towel
- **steps[0].caption** Pour a different amount of water into each bottle.
- **steps[1].caption** Guess: which bottle will sound highest?
- **steps[2].caption** Blow gently across the top.
- **steps[3].caption** Put them in order, from low to high.
- **steps[4].caption** Play a tune. Rest between notes.
- **safety** Plastic bottles only, never glass. An adult watches the water the whole time, and spills are wiped up straight away so nobody slips. If there is an accident, call your local emergency number.
- **forAdult** In the story, Lily reaches her goal by practising a little every day and ticking a box each time. This activity is about music too, and about finding out how a sound changes. When you blow gently across the open top of a bottle, the air inside vibrates and makes a note. More water leaves less air inside, so the note goes higher. (Tapping a plastic bottle gives only a soft, dull sound, so here we blow. Clear, ringing tapped notes come from glass bottles, which this activity leaves out.) Once each bottle makes a note, the child puts them in order from lowest to highest and checks by playing them in a row. Then try a simple tune, or make one up. Remind them to rest and breathe between notes. If a note is not quite right, pour in or out a little water and try again. Praise the patience (“you tried it again and again”), not a perfect tune. Twenty-five minutes is about right. To make it easier, use two bottles: one nearly empty, one nearly full. To make it harder, tune five or more bottles, or draw a chart of which bottle played which note. And if the child likes it, give them a sheet with a few boxes to tick, like Lily’s.
- **inClass** Groups of four, each with four bottles on a towel and one adult watching the water. Each child fills, tunes and blows only their own bottle, so nobody shares a mouthpiece. At the end, each band plays its notes for the class, and the class guesses which bottle has the most water.

- **p2** “Why must we go?” Long-Legs asked a big stork beside him. “I like it here.” The big stork clattered her beak. “Soon the cold will come,” she said. “The frogs will hide, and the fields will be bare. Where we are going, there will be food.”
- **p3** Long-Legs flapped his wings as hard as he could. Flap, flap, flap. He rose a little, then sank down again, puffing. “I can’t flap all the way,” he said. “It’s too far.” “Nobody flaps all the way,” said the big stork. “Wait for the sun.”
- **p4** So the next morning, the storks waited. Nobody flew while the ground was cold. Then the sun climbed higher and warmed the fields. Over the warm ground, the air began to rise, slowly, like an invisible tower. Nobody could see it. But the storks could feel it.
- **p5** “Now!” said the big stork. She opened her wide wings and stepped into the rising air. Up she went, round and round, without a single flap. Long-Legs opened his wings too. The warm air lifted him like a hand. Round and round, and up and up.
- **p6** At the top, the storks were tiny specks in the sky. Then they stretched out their necks and glided south. Down and down they slid, a long, long way. At last they found the next warm tower. Round and round, and up and up again. Flying was easy, if you knew where the warm air was.
- **p7** One day, they came to the edge of the sea. The water shone blue and flat, all the way to the sky. “Let’s fly straight across,” said Long-Legs. “It’s shorter.” The big stork shook her head. “No warm air rises over the sea,” she said. “We follow the land.” So the flock turned and flew along the shore.
- **p8** Every evening, when the air grew cool, the storks came down to rest. They stood in fields and in tall trees, one leg tucked up, beaks resting on their chests. Long-Legs was tired right down to his feathers. Above him, the stars came out. Tomorrow there would be more sky.
- **p9** Day after day they flew, and the land below them changed. First there were dark forests and rivers. Then came brown hills, dry fields and small white houses. The days grew warm again. Long-Legs’ wings grew stronger, and he stopped puffing.
- **p10** One afternoon they saw a green valley, full of shining fishponds. “Down!” called the big stork. Thousands of storks spiralled down together, like a great white whirl. They landed by the water, and they ate and ate. Long-Legs had never seen so many storks in one place.
- **p11** On a hill above the valley sat a girl and her grandfather. The girl held the binoculars, and her grandfather held a notebook. “There are so many!” she said. “How can we ever count them?” “In fives,” said her grandfather. He drew four little lines and one line across them. “Five. Now you try.”
- **p12** The girl counted and drew, counted and drew. Four lines and one across, four lines and one across. Her page filled up with little gates. Down by a pond, a young stork stood on one leg and looked up at the hill. The girl lowered her binoculars and waved.
- **p13** In the morning, the valley was cool and misty. The storks waited. Then the sun warmed the ground, and the air began to rise. This time, Long-Legs felt it first. He opened his wings and stepped in. Round and round, and up and up, and the other storks rose behind him.
- **p14** Up on the hill, the girl watched the storks turn into a slow white tower. Then the tower drifted south and was gone. “Will they come back?” she asked. “In spring,” said her grandfather. “Storks fly back north the same way.” The girl drew one more stork in her notebook. One spring, Long-Legs would come back too.

### Activity

- **title** Sky Tally
- **goal** Count with tally marks while watching nature.
- **materials[0]** A sheet of paper
- **materials[1]** A pencil
- **steps[0].caption** Draw three columns: small, medium, big. Guess which wins.

- **safety** Sit in the shade at a cool time of day, with a hat and water. Never look straight at the sun. Watch the birds; do not touch or feed them. If there is an accident, call your local emergency number.
- **forAdult** In the story, Long-Legs learns why storks leave the north when the cold comes, and how they make such a long journey. White storks do not flap all the way. They wait until the sun has warmed the ground, ride the warm air that rises from it in slow circles, then glide to the next rising column. No such warm air forms over the open sea, so they follow the land, and they fly only by day. In autumn, from about August, huge numbers pass over Israel, and green valleys with fishponds are important resting places. Young storks can make their first journey without their parents. Most of them then stay in the south for their first summers and come back north only after a few years, when they are ready to nest. This activity turns the girl’s counting on the hill into a real watch. Choose a cool time of day (in hot months, before ten in the morning or after four in the afternoon), sit in the shade with hats and water, and watch the sky for about fifteen minutes. Before you start, the child draws three columns for small, medium and big birds and guesses which will win. Each bird that flies past gets one line in its column. Every fifth line goes across the other four, making a “gate”. At the end, count the gates in fives (five, ten, fifteen) and then the lines left over. Was the guess right? Praise careful watching and steady counting (“you didn’t miss one”). To make it easier, use one column only. To make it harder, add a column for birds flying in a group, or watch again on another day and compare.
- **inClass** Groups of four sit together outside, in the shade. Each child watches one part of the sky (towards the building, the trees, the road, the open sky) and keeps their own tally. At the end, the group adds its four tallies together, counting the gates in fives, and the class compares groups.

## C11 · Meet Me at Four

Age group: 6-8 · Felix, Grandpa, Felix’s friend

### Book

- **p1** Felix was always late. He was late for football in the square. He was late for lunch at Grandpa’s. Once he was so late for a party that the cake was all gone. The trouble was the big clock on the tower. Felix looked up at it every day. Two hands, twelve marks, and no idea.
- **p2** Felix’s grandpa was the clock keeper. Every week he climbed the tower and wound up the clock, so that it never stopped. One spring afternoon, he unlocked the little tower door and held out his hand. “Come up with me,” he said. “It’s time you met the clock.”
- **p3** The stairs were narrow, and they went round and round. Up and up they climbed, past little windows full of sky. Felix counted the steps, lost count and started again. At the very top was a small room with a wooden floor. And the room was ticking.
- **p4** In the middle of the room, a few big wheels turned slowly, each one pushing the next. Tick… tock. Tick… tock. One whole wall was the back of the giant clock face. The sun shone through it, and the twelve marks glowed. Felix stood very still. It was like standing inside the clock’s heart.
- **p5** Grandpa fitted a big iron handle into the clock and wound it round and round, until his cheeks were pink. Then he fetched an old wooden clock face from his workbench. It had twelve marks, like the big one, and two hands that you could move. “Now,” said Grandpa, “let’s learn to read it.”
- **p6** “The short hand tells the hour,” said Grandpa. He moved it from mark to mark, counting from the top: one, two, three. Then he pointed the long hand straight up. “When the long hand points straight up, it’s o’clock. Exactly. So this is three o’clock.” Felix nodded slowly.
- **p7** Then Grandpa turned the long hand round until it pointed straight down. The short hand moved too, a little, until it sat halfway between three and four. “Straight down is half past,” said Grandpa. “This is half past three.” Felix closed his eyes and said it like a song. “Straight up, o’clock. Straight down, half past.”
- **p8** Felix practised all week. He read the kitchen clock at breakfast and the tower clock from his window. At Grandpa’s table he shouted, “Twelve o’clock!” Grandpa raised one eyebrow. “Which hand is the short one?” Felix looked again. “Oh. Ten o’clock.” They both laughed. “Straight up, o’clock,” Felix sang. “Straight down, half past.”
- **p9** At the end of the week, Felix’s friend came running across the square. She was hiding something behind her back. “Meet me at the fountain at four,” she said. “I want to show you something. Don’t be late!” Felix gulped. Four o’clock. He looked up at the tower. He could do this.
- **p10** After lunch, Felix played marbles in front of the bakery. Click, click, roll. He almost forgot the time. Then he stopped and looked up. The long hand pointed straight down. The short hand sat halfway between three and four. “Half past three,” said Felix. “Half an hour to go.”
- **p11** Half an hour was long enough for one more game. But Felix put his marbles in his pocket anyway. He walked across the square to the fountain and sat down on its stone edge. The water splashed. The pigeons strutted. And the long hand climbed, very slowly, up and up.
- **p12** Felix watched it the whole time. Nearly up… nearly up… What if he had read it wrong? What if she forgot? His heart was thumping. He thought of the big wheels inside the tower. Each one pushed the next, all the way round to four.
- **p13** Then the long hand pointed straight up, and the short hand pointed at four. Bong, went the clock. Bong. Bong. Bong. It was so loud that all the pigeons flew up at once. On the very last bong, his friend came running round the corner. “Felix,” she puffed. “You’re here first!”
- **p14** From behind her back she pulled out a paper windmill, red and yellow, and it spun in the breeze. High up in the tower window, Grandpa leaned out and waved. Felix waved back. Then he looked up at the clock. Two hands, twelve marks, and he knew exactly what they said.

### Activity

- **title** How Long Is a Minute?
- **goal** Feel and estimate how long one minute lasts.
- **materials[0]** A kitchen timer or a phone timer (held by the grown-up)
- **materials[1]** A sheet of paper
- **materials[2]** A pencil
- **steps[0].caption** Close your eyes. Say “now” after one minute.
- **steps[1].caption** Too early or too late? Mark it.
- **steps[2].caption** Try again. Count slowly: one-and-two-and…
- **steps[3].caption** How many hops fit in a minute?
- **steps[4].caption** Guess first: how many fruits can you name?
- **safety** Hop in a clear space, away from stairs, glass and sharp furniture corners. The grown-up holds the phone or timer. If there is an accident, call your local emergency number.
- **forAdult** In the story, Felix learns to read the big clock: the short hand tells the hour, the long hand straight up means o’clock, and straight down means half past, with the short hand halfway between two hours. This activity gives the child a feel for time itself, because reading a clock means more once you know how long a minute is. Start a timer (keep the screen out of sight) and ask the child to close their eyes and say “now” when they think one minute has passed. Show them how close they were, and let them mark it on a line drawn on paper, with “too early” at one end and “too late” at the other. Then try again, counting slowly, “one-and-two-and-three”, and see whether the guess gets closer. Finally, find out what fits into one minute: how many hops on one foot, how many names of fruit. Always ask for a guess first, then count, and compare the guess with what really happened. Praise careful guessing and trying again (“you got closer!”), not being “clever”. To make it easier, use half a minute. To make it harder, try two minutes, or guess how long an everyday job takes, such as putting on shoes, and time it. If you have a clock with hands at home, look at it together at o’clock and half past during the day.
- **inClass** Everyone sits, closes their eyes, and stands up quietly when they think one minute is over. The teacher watches the timer and calls out when the real minute ends, so children can see who stood up early and who stood up late. Then pairs take turns: one hops, one counts, and the teacher calls start and stop for the whole class.

## C12 · Words and Pictures

Age group: 6-8 · Tamara, her little brother, Mum, the nurse, the boy in the next bed

### Book

- **p1** Tamara’s little brother had fallen off a swing and broken his leg. Now he lay in a hospital bed with a big white cast and a very grumpy face. “I’m bored,” he said. “I want to go home.” Tamara stood by the bed. She was hiding something behind her back.
- **p2** It was her favourite book, the one about the little tortoise. Tamara had just learned to read, and she had practised all week. She sat on the edge of the bed and opened the book on her knees. “I’ll read it to you,” she said. Her brother groaned. But he looked at the cover all the same.
- **p3** Tamara put her finger under the first word. “Once… upon… a… time,” she read slowly. “There… was… a… little… tortoise.” Her finger moved along, word by word. Her brother wriggled. “You’re so slow,” he said. Tamara frowned and kept going.
- **p4** “The… tortoise… climbed… up… the… hill. Then… he… did… an… enormous…” Tamara came to a long, long word, and she stopped. She tried the beginning. She tried the end. The word just sat there on the page, too big to get past.
- **p5** “Somersault,” shouted her brother. “He did an enormous somersault.” He knew the whole book by heart. Tamara slammed the book shut. “I was going to read it!” she said. “I can do it myself.” Now there were two grumpy faces on the bed.
- **p6** For a while, nobody said anything. A trolley rattled past in the corridor. Rain tapped on the window. Tamara looked at the book on her knees. Her brother looked at the cast on his leg. Then he said, very quietly, “I like the pictures best anyway.”
- **p7** Tamara opened the book again. In the picture, the tortoise was tumbling down the hill. A rabbit was laughing, and a bird was flapping away in surprise. “You could tell the pictures,” said Tamara slowly. “And I’ll read the words.” Her brother thought about it. “Deal,” he said.
- **p8** So they took turns. Tamara read the words, with her finger under each one. Then her brother read the pictures, with his finger on each one. “Look, the rabbit is laughing so much that he’s fallen over. And the bird dropped its worm!” He saw things Tamara had never even noticed.
- **p9** In the next bed lay a boy with his arm in a sling. He had been pretending to sleep. Now one of his eyes was open. Then both eyes. Then he sat up. “What happens next?” he asked. Tamara turned the book so he could see. “Listen,” she said.
- **p10** The tortoise rolled into a pond, splash, and climbed out with a frog on his head. Tamara read it, and her brother pulled the frog’s surprised face. The boy in the next bed laughed so much that Mum came back from the corridor with the water jug. “What’s so funny in here?” she asked. Then she sat down to listen.
- **p11** Then the nurse came in to check the cast. She looked at the leg. She looked at the book. “Don’t stop because of me,” she said. So Tamara didn’t stop. The nurse wrote on her clipboard and listened with one ear. Then she pulled up a stool and listened with both.
- **p12** Soon Tamara came to another long word. This time her brother clapped both hands over his mouth and waited. Tamara broke the word into little pieces and said them one by one. “Cel… e… bra… tion. Celebration!” Everybody clapped, even the boy in the next bed, with his one good hand.
- **p13** On the last page, the animals were having a party on the hill, with the little tortoise in the middle. Her brother told every single thing in the picture, right down to the snail at the very edge. The snail was late. Outside, the sky had turned dark. The city lights came on, one by one.
- **p14** Around the bed sat three listeners now: the boy from the next bed, Mum and the nurse. Tamara closed the book. “Again!” said her brother. The boy held out a book of his own. “Then mine?” he asked. Tamara looked round at all their faces. Nobody on the whole ward was grumpy any more.

- **forAdult** In the story, Tamara is just learning to read, and her little brother knows the book by heart. ### Book

- **p1** All night long, the winter wind blew round the village. It howled in the chimneys and rattled the shutters. Branches knocked against the roofs. Diana lay in bed and listened, with her blanket pulled up to her nose. At last, very late, the wind grew tired, and the village went quiet.
- **p2** In the morning, the sky was clean and pale. Diana looked out of her window. The street was covered in branches, big ones and small ones, and puddles shone everywhere. At the far end stood the little kindergarten. And right across its gate lay a huge branch from the old pine tree.

- **p14** The next morning, Diana walked past the kindergarten on her way to school. On the gate hung five drawings, one for each helper. A boy with a broom. A girl in a wheelchair, calling out loud. Two boys in yellow boots, in two drawings that looked exactly the same. And a girl in a red coat, pushing with all her might. Diana smiled all the way to school.

### Activity

- **title** The Paper Bridge
- **goal** Discover that folding paper makes it stronger.
- **materials[0]** A few sheets of paper
- **materials[1]** Two books of the same height
- **materials[2]** Pebbles or coins (counted back afterwards)
- **steps[0].caption** Lay a flat sheet across two books.
- **steps[1].caption** Guess first. Then add pebbles, one by one.
- **steps[2].caption** Fold a new sheet into a zigzag.
- **steps[3].caption** Agree on your bridge. Test again.
- **steps[4].caption** Which held more? Count them back.
- **safety** Pebbles and coins never go in the mouth. Count them all back into a cup at the end, and wash hands afterwards. If there is an accident, call your local emergency number.
- **forAdult** In the story, Celine and Rani each have a good idea, a sail that moves the raft and a floor that keeps it steady, but each idea fails alone. They stop arguing when each one asks the other to explain, and together they make a raft that neither could make alone. This activity asks two children to do the same with a real problem: how to make a sheet of paper hold more weight. Lay two books of the same height a hand’s width apart and put a flat sheet of paper across them. Before adding anything, both children guess how many pebbles or coins it will hold. Add them one at a time to the middle of the paper and count until the bridge sags and they slide off. Then fold a new sheet back and forth into a zigzag, like a fan. To make it easier, the adult folds the zigzag and the children test it. To make it harder, try narrow and wide folds, or roll the paper into a tube. At the end, count every coin back into the cup together.

- **title** Warning-Sign Detectives
- **goal** Recognise the warning pictures on product labels.
- **materials[0]** Two or three of your own closed cleaning products (held only by the grown-up)

- **materials[2]** A red crayon

The lesson for the child is simple: never taste or open a bottle, a box or a pill you do not know. Leave it where it is, and ask a grown-up first. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention lists keeping harmful household products out of reach among the safety goals for children of six to eight, and the American Academy of Pediatrics notes that most poisonings happen when caregivers are at home but not paying attention.
