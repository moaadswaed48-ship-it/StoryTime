## A. CDC milestone checklists (revised 2022 CDC/AAP, "75% of children can do this by this age")

### By 2 years  https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html
- Social/emotional: notices when others are hurt or upset (pauses or looks sad when someone cries); looks at your face to see how to react in a new situation.
- Language: points to things in a book when asked ("Where is the bear?"); says at least two words together ("More milk"); points to at least two body parts; uses more gestures than waving/pointing (blows a kiss, nods yes).
- Cognitive: holds something in one hand while using the other; tries switches/knobs/buttons; plays with more than one toy at once (toy food on a plate).
- Motor: kicks a ball; runs; walks up a few stairs; eats with a spoon.
### By 3 years  https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html
- Social/emotional: calms within 10 minutes after caregiver leaves (childcare drop-off); notices other children and joins them to play.
- Language: conversation of at least two back-and-forth exchanges; asks who/what/where/why; says what action is happening in a picture or book ("running", "eating"); says first name; talks well enough for others to understand most of the time.
- Cognitive: draws a circle when shown; avoids touching hot objects when warned.
- Motor: strings large beads; puts on some clothes by himself (loose pants, jacket); uses a fork.

## B. CDC "Positive Parenting Tips" stages (the owner's hypothesis source)
- CDC publishes stages: Infants, Toddlers (1-2), Toddlers (2-3), Preschoolers (3-5), Middle childhood (6-8), (9-11), Teens. Note "2-3" and "3-5" share the age-3 boundary; the CDC pages for 2-3 returned 404 on the current URL (page moved) so the 2-3 stage text is unverified here, but the CDC milestone checklists (above) are by 2, 3, 4, 5 years.
- Preschoolers 3-5 (mirror of CDC page): https://www.restoredcdc.org/www.cdc.gov/child-development/positive-parenting-tips/preschooler-3-5-years.html (live: https://www.cdc.gov/child-development/positive-parenting-tips/preschooler-3-5-years.html)
  - Children should be able to help dress/undress, recall part of a story, sing songs; play with other children, ride a tricycle, use safety scissors; world opens up, they ask about things; focus more on adults and children outside the family.
  - Tips: keep reading, library/bookstore; simple chores; clear consistent discipline, say what to do instead of "no"; speak in complete sentences and "grown up" words; help through problem-solving steps when upset; limited simple choices (what to wear, snack); encourage play with other children "to learn the value of sharing and friendship".
  - Safety: why to stay out of traffic; do not play in the street or run after stray balls; tricycle on sidewalk with helmet; watch at all times outside; stranger safety. Healthy habits: eat meals together; keep TV out of the bedroom.
- Middle childhood 6-8 (mirror): https://www.restoredcdc.org/www.cdc.gov/child-development/positive-parenting-tips/middle-childhood-6-8-years.html (live URL: https://www.cdc.gov/child-development/positive-parenting-tips/middle-childhood-6-8-years.html)
  - Dress themselves, catch a ball with hands only, tie shoes; independence from family more important; starting school brings contact with the larger world; friendships more and more important; critical time to build confidence (friends, schoolwork, sports).
  - Emotional/social: more independence; start to think about the future; understand more about their place in the world; attention to friendships and teamwork; want to be liked and accepted.
  - Thinking: rapid growth of mental skills; better ways to describe experiences and talk about thoughts and feelings; less focus on self, more concern for others.
  - Tips: talk about respecting others and helping people in need; keep reading to the child, take turns reading to each other once the child reads; encourage solving small disagreements on their own; join groups, volunteering.
  - Responsibility: affection, recognise accomplishments; household tasks (set the table); learn patience by letting others go first or finishing a task before play; think about consequences; clear rules (TV time, bedtime); discipline to guide not to shame; praise effort ("you worked hard") over traits ("you are smart"); set own achievable goals.
  - Safety: traffic/walking to school/bike; water safety; supervise climbing; how to ask for help; harmful products and firearms out of reach; car seats.

## C. Screen time and video (question 4)
- AAP 2026 policy "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents", Pediatrics Feb 2026 (online Jan 20 2026), doi:10.1542/peds.2025-075320 (cited by https://www.contemporarypediatrics.com/view/aap-calls-for-system-level-changes-to-improve-children-s-digital-media-environments ; DOI itself not opened = unverified). Family version: https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Helping-Kids-Thrive-in-a-Digital-World-AAP-Policy-Explained.aspx
  - Shift from strict screen-time limits to "quality, context and conversation" (healthychildren page above, fetched).
  - Early childhood 0-5: heavy solo use can slow language and social development; linked to poor sleep, more angry outbursts, less patience and self-control; best way is to watch together; one shared family device is easier than personal devices; under-18-months learn best from real-world interaction; keep screens out of bedrooms and the hour before bed. (same URL)
  - School age 6-12: misuse linked to poorer school performance, weaker attention control, less exercise; watch/play alongside, delay personal tablets; choose content that builds social-emotional skills, supports reading and math, or encourages free play; be wary of autoplay, endless scroll, targeted ads. (same URL)
- AAP numeric limit: 2016 statement kept "1 hour or less a day of high-quality programming" for ages 2-5, none under 18 months except video chat, co-view for 18-24 months. Source for wording: Harvard Health summary https://www.harvardhealth.org/blog/new-expert-recommendations-on-media-use-and-children-2016102510564 (fetched). CHOC (hospital, secondary) says the Feb 2026 AAP guidance keeps "one hour of high-quality content daily for ages 2-5" with co-viewing: https://health.choc.org/updated-aap-recommendations-for-screen-time/ . Whether the 2026 policy text itself keeps the 1-hour figure is UNVERIFIED (I read only the explainer, which gives no number).
- WHO 2019 (children under 5): under 1 year screen time not recommended; age 1 sedentary screen time not recommended; age 2 no more than 1 hour, "less is better"; ages 3-4 "no more than 1 hour; less is better"; "When sedentary, engaging in reading and storytelling with a caregiver is encouraged"; not restrained or sitting for extended periods more than 1 hour at a time; sleep 11-14 h (1-2 y), 10-13 h (3-4 y); 3-4 y 180 min physical activity incl. 60 min moderate-vigorous. Sources: https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more (fetched) ; guideline https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550536
- Implication for film length (my inference, not a source claim): the 1-hour daily cap is for ALL screen use, so a single film should be a small fraction of it. Short episodes (about 5-10 min for 2-3, up to about 10-15 for 3-5) with a natural end, no autoplay, a built-in "stop" moment and a parent prompt for co-viewing. 6-8 can handle longer but AAP gives no numeric cap for them.

## D. National curricula (borders and teaching goals)

### D1. England EYFS "Development Matters" (DfE non-statutory guidance, updated Sep 2023, 128 pp, fetched and read as PDF)
URL page: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/development-matters--2 ; PDF: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/64e6002a20ae890014f26cbc/DfE_Development_Matters_Report_Sep2023.pdf (cited below as DM, p. = section). Statutory EYFS 2025 framework (birth-5; 3 prime areas + 4 specific areas; 17 early learning goals): https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687105a381dd8f70f5de3ea9/EYFS_framework_for_group_and_school_based_providers_.pdf (returned HTTP 410 to my fetch; facts from search summary = secondary).
- BORDERS: Development Matters uses bands "Birth to three", "3 and 4-year-olds", "Reception (age 4-5)". It does NOT have a separate 2-3 band, and treats 3-4 as one band and 4-5 as another. (DM, all sections)
- Language (DM, Communication and Language):
  - By about 2: up to 50 words, starting to join two or three words ("more milk"); by about 3: around 300 words including words for time, space, function; links up to 5 words; uses pronouns, plurals, prepositions; follows instructions with three key words ("Can you wash dolly's face?").
  - 2-3 y: "Listen to simple stories and understand what is happening, with the help of the pictures."; shares picture books daily; wordless books encourage conversation; "four comments to every question".
  - 3-4 y: sings a large repertoire of songs; knows many rhymes, can talk about familiar books and "tell a long story"; sentences of four to six words by around 4; sentences joined with "because", "or", "and"; uses past and future tense; answers simple "why" questions; may still say "swimmed", "runned"; mispronounces r, j, th, ch, sh and long words like "hippopotamus"; can express a point of view and debate with words, hold conversation for many turns.
  - Reception (4-5): understand how and why to listen; learn new vocabulary and use it through the day; connect ideas with connectives; describe events in detail with sequence; engage in storytimes (at least one a day); retell story in own words after deep familiarity; listen to rhymes paying attention to how they sound.
  - Attention (DM): at 2-3 children "generally focus on an activity of their own choice and find it difficult to be directed by an adult" and "can easily be distracted"; around age 3 can shift from one task to another if you get attention with their name.
- Personal, social, emotional (DM, PSED):
  - 2-3: "effortful control", e.g. waiting for a turn, resisting grabbing; "me do it" independence and tantrums; say feelings in words ("I'm sad because..."); notice differences (skin colour, hair, gender, disability, religion); "Safely explore emotions beyond their normal range through play and stories"; toilet learning; manage separation from parent.
  - 3-4: play with others, elaborate play; find solutions to conflicts and rivalries; increasingly follow rules and understand why; remember rules without reminders; be assertive in appropriate ways; talk about feelings with words happy, sad, angry, worried; understand gradually how others feel; sense of responsibility and membership of a community; more independence in tooth-brushing, toilet, hand-washing; make healthy choices about food, drink, activity.
  - Reception: see self as valuable; build constructive, respectful relationships; express feelings and consider feelings of others; resilience and perseverance; moderate own feelings; think about others' perspectives; personal hygiene; know factors for health: physical activity, healthy eating, toothbrushing, "sensible amounts of screen time", sleep routine, being a safe pedestrian.
- Literacy / maths (DM): 3-4: spot and suggest rhymes, count/clap syllables, same initial sounds, know some letters of own name; Reception: read letters by sound, blend sounds into short words, write name and some letters, read simple phrases. Maths: 2-3 finger rhymes with numbers, compare "lots/more/same", notice and arrange patterns; 3-4: say one number per item 1-5, know last number counted is the total, subitise, finger numbers to 5, compare quantities "more than/fewer than", talk about 2D and 3D shapes, extend ABAB patterns; Reception: count beyond ten, numbers to 10 and their composition, number bonds 0-5.

## E. Arabic diglossia (question 6, Arabic)
- Shahbari-Kassem, Asli-Badarneh, Hende, Roby-Bayaa (2024), "Reading stories in Arabic: the impact of lexico-phonological and diglossic distance level on comprehension and receptive and productive vocabulary among Arab kindergarten children", Frontiers in Education 9, doi 10.3389/feduc.2024.1394024 (fetched, first 100k chars read). https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1394024/full
  - N=139 Israeli-Palestinian kindergartners (mean age about 5.7 y), 3-month programme, weekly 20-min sessions, four storybooks ordered from Spoken Arabic (SpA) toward Standard Arabic (StA); intervention vs comparison group; authors state design is not causal.
  - Finding: comprehension and vocabulary were best on SpA stories ("stories told entirely in Spoken Arabic were the most effective"), worst on StA-only stories; scores fell as StA share rose; the intervention group had higher receptive vocabulary and overall gains "especially with more StA content". Authors recommend reading "systematically and gradually" from mostly SpA toward StA, choosing stories by lexical readability and explicitly teaching cognate and unique StA words, also at home. Code-switching between SpA and StA is described in the literature review as a third approach, not tested.
  - Limits stated: unequal teacher training, no control for prior vocabulary.
- Via that paper's literature review (secondary, not opened): Feitelson et al. 1993 showed kindergarten exposure to StA through storybook oral reading supports literary-language development; Saiegh-Haddad (2023), "Embracing diglossia in early literacy education in Arabic: a pilot intervention study with kindergarten children", Oxford Review of Education 49, 48-68; Korat et al. (2007, 2012) on book-reading mediation among Arabic-speaking families. Another 2025 mini-review argues diglossia is a normal context, not a deficit (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1518728/epub, not opened). All these: UNVERIFIED by me beyond the Frontiers article.
- Product implication (my inference): for 2-3 and 3-5, narration in simplified MSA with dialect-near vocabulary and dialogue lines in a light spoken register (code-switch), glossing a few "unique" MSA words by repetition/pictures; ramp MSA share up with age; offer the dialect choice (Levantine/Palestinian first as the owner's audience is probably Israel; confirm).

## F. Evidence on shared reading and SEL (question 7), verified from search summaries unless tagged
1. Mol & Bus (2011), "To read or not to read: a meta-analysis of print exposure from infancy to early adulthood", Psychological Bulletin 137(2), 267-296, doi 10.1037/a0021890. Finding: moderate to strong correlations between print exposure and reading comprehension, technical reading, spelling; reciprocal cycle. Abstract-level only. https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A2899523/view (not opened in full; tag: abstract only).
2. Durlak, Weissberg, Dymnicki, Taylor & Schellinger (2011), "The impact of enhancing students' social and emotional learning: a meta-analysis of school-based universal interventions", Child Development 82(1), 405-432. 213 programmes, 270,034 students; SEL participants gained about 11 percentile points on achievement (only 37 of 213 studies measured achievement); better outcomes with SAFE practices (sequenced, active, focused, explicit). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21291449/
3. Jones, Greenberg & Crowley (2015), "Early social-emotional functioning and public health: the relationship between kindergarten social competence and future wellness", American Journal of Public Health 105(11), 2283-2290, doi 10.2105/AJPH.2015.302630. Fast Track sample; teacher-rated prosocial skills in kindergarten significantly associated with education, employment, crime, substance use and mental-health outcomes 13-19 years later, adjusted for child/family factors. Effect sizes not read. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4605168
4. Taylor, Oberle, Durlak & Weissberg (2017), "Promoting positive youth development through school-based SEL interventions: a meta-analysis of follow-up effects", Child Development 88(4), 1156-1171. 82 universal interventions, 97,406 K-12 students, follow-up 6 months to 18 years; participants better on social-emotional skills and well-being; academic gain of 13 percentile points at follow-up rests on only 8 studies; effects weaker at later follow-up; two authors affiliated with CASEL (advocacy body) = possible bias. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28685826/ (abstract-level plus summaries).
5. Mol, Bus, de Jong & Smeets (2008), "Added value of dialogic parent-child book readings: a meta-analysis", Early Education and Development 19, 7-26, doi 10.1080/10409280701838603. 16 studies; dialogic reading adds to ordinary shared reading for EXPRESSIVE vocabulary (about 4% variance, secondary); not significant for receptive; benefit concentrated in typically developing 4-year-olds, smaller for at-risk families. https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2899524/view (abstract-level, not opened).
6. Moffitt et al. (2011), "A gradient of childhood self-control predicts health, wealth, and public safety", PNAS 108(7), 2693-2698, doi 10.1073/pnas.1010076108. About 1,000 children followed to age 32; childhood self-control (measured ages 3-11) predicts health, substance dependence, finances, crime, independent of IQ and social class; observational. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3041102 (search summary).
7. Lee, Talwar, McCarthy, Ross, Evans & Arruda (2014), "Can classic moral stories promote honesty in children?", Psychological Science 25(8), doi 10.1177/0956797614536401. 268 children aged 3-7 (temptation-to-peek task). Pinocchio and The Boy Who Cried Wolf did NOT reduce lying; George Washington and the cherry tree (honesty rewarded) did increase truth-telling; the version ending with a negative note failed. Lesson: show positive outcome of honesty, not punishment of lying. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0956797614536401 ; summary https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/moral-tales-with-positive-outcomes-motivate-kids-to-be-honest.html . Single lab; long-term effects untested (authors say so).
8. Fehr, Bernhard & Rockenbach (2008), "Egalitarianism in young children", Nature 454, 1079-1083, doi 10.1038/nature07155. 229 Swiss children 3-8: young children mostly choose selfishly; inequality aversion grows strongly 3-8; most 7-8 y olds prefer fair splits but favour their in-group. Implication: "sharing" as a goal is realistic from about 3-4 as taking turns/small gestures, fairness as a concept from about 6-8. https://www.doi.org/10.1038/NATURE07155
9. Wellman, Cross & Watson (2001), "Meta-analysis of theory-of-mind development: the truth about false belief", Child Development 72(3), 655-684, doi 10.1111/1467-8624.00304. 178 studies; children move from below to above chance on false-belief tasks across the preschool years, consensus about age 4; conceptual change, not task artifact. 10. Fenson et al. (1994), MacArthur CDI norms (monograph, Society for Research in Child Development): about 1,800 parents; toddler form N=1,130 (16-30 months); large true variability between children of the same age. https://mb-cdi.stanford.edu/documents/Fensonetal2000.pdf (summary only). Use: vocabulary numbers in this file are averages with wide spread.
11. Israeli storybook studies (titles from search only, UNVERIFIED content): Aram (2006) Reading and Writing, "Early literacy interventions among low SES preschoolers: the relative roles of storybook reading and alphabetic skills activities"; Levin & Aram (2012) Reading and Writing 25, 217-249, "Mother-child joint writing and storybook reading and their effects on kindergartners' literacy: an intervention study"; Aram & Biron (2004).

## G. Story norms and read-aloud conventions (question 5)
- Publishing (single professional source, tagged "personal references, not official", Boyds Mills / Highlights Foundation article; https://boydsmills.org/2019/04/08/picture-book-word-count-and-other-rules-meant-to-be-broken/ ): board books ages 0-3 under 50 words, 8-24 pages; board books ages 2-5 under 200 words, 24-32 pages; picture books ages 4-8 (PreK-2) at or under 500 words, 32 pages; picture books 7-10 (grades 2-4) at or under 1,000 words, 32-48 pages; nonfiction may be double; the author's own picture books ran 147-1,400 words.
- Cross-check from a search of several self-publishing and writing guides (blogs, tag: weak, they disagree): board books 0-100 words; picture books for 3-5 about 200-1,000 (often 400-800); early readers about 300-2,500; chapter books about 3,000-15,000. Source list: https://selfpublishing.com/how-many-words-in-a-childrens-book/ ; https://writeforkids.online/childrens-book-word-counts-by-age-group/ (not opened). Treat as ranges, not rules.
- AAP early-literacy toolkit "Choosing books for children and families" (fetched): https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/early-childhood/early-childhood-health-and-development/early-literacy/early-literacy-toolkit/choosing-books-for-children-and-families/
  - Ages 1-2: simple text with repetitive phrases or rhymes "that can help toddlers predict what comes next"; board books; topics: daily routines like bedtime, naming and handling emotions, silly books. About 30 months: children can often turn paper pages and enjoy picture books.
  - Ages 3-5: longer stories; text children can start to memorize and fill in; counting, alphabet and rhyming books; familiar topics (school, friendship, feelings); unfamiliar topics build knowledge and empathy; match their interests (animals, transport, science).
  - Early readers: "simple but engaging language they can master independently".
- Reach Out and Read (RoR) Milestones, adapted from AAP Bright Futures 4th ed., fetched and read: https://reachoutandread.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/ROR-Parent-Milestones-8.5x11.pdf
  - 15-18 months: board books, simple rhymes, predictable text, teeth-brushing and daily-routine books; "a few minutes every day is enough"; child need not sit still, "they are listening, even if they are moving around".
  - 24 and 30 months: board/paperback, search-and-find, counting and alphabet, potty-training books, books on the child's current interests; point to colours, count objects; ask what/where questions; point out letters and numbers.
  - 3 years: books on sharing, younger siblings, feelings; ask what and where questions.
  - 4 years: books about going to school, family and friendship relations; ask why / how / "would you do that?" questions; encourage own opinions ("What would you do?").
  - 5 years: ask "what happens next" in familiar stories; point out letters in the child's name; relate story to child's life; let the child tell the story from the pictures.
  - NOTE for borders: RoR/Bright Futures separate 24-30 months, 3 y, 4 y and 5 y; the questions shift from what/where (3) to why/how/would-you (4).
- Toddler repetition and attention (KidsHealth/Nemours, a paediatric hospital resource; tag: hospital source): toddlers love repetition (same book for weeks); OK if the child cannot sit through an entire book; 24-36 months: repetitive, easy-to-memorise text so they can "read" along; pause so the child finishes familiar phrases or rhymes. https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/reading-toddler.html
- CHOP/Reach Out and Read (secondary): toddlers: rhyming and repetition books best for language; preschoolers like stories related to their own life. https://www.chop.edu/news/make-most-story-time-tips-when-where-and-what-read-young-children
- DM (England, above) for read-aloud: "Share picture books every day"; wordless books prompt conversation; ask few questions ("four comments to every question"); reception: at least one storytime a day, re-read favourite stories, use different voices for narrator and characters, pause before the rhyming word.
- TV attention research (secondary summary; original Anderson et al. 1981 not opened): preschoolers watching age-suitable TV in a room with toys look at and away from the screen about 150 times an hour, average look about 13 seconds; attention depends on whether content is comprehensible (looking drops with foreign language, backwards speech, scrambled shots); children's viewing patterns converge with age; very few studies cover ages 2-3. Search page: https://scholars.uky.edu/es/publications/preschool-childrens-visual-attention-to-attributes-of-television/ . NO source found for an exact "minutes of attention per age" rule: the popular "age plus 1 minute" rule is a folk rule, do NOT cite it.
- What this means for film length (inference, my judgement): no trusted source gives an official ideal clip length. Take the WHO/AAP cap (1 h/day total for 2-5, less is better) and the attention evidence (comprehensibility drives attention) and make the 2-3 film a series of 3-6 min segments with a repeated structure; 3-5: one story of about 8-12 min; 6-8: 12-20 min, never autoplay. These numbers are PROPOSALS to test, not findings.

## H. Language-specific notes (question 6)
### Arabic
- See section E: best evidence (one study, kindergarten, Israel) says dialect-heavy stories are understood best, MSA-only worst; graded move toward MSA works; explicit teaching of MSA words helps.
- Diacritics (tashkeel): Arabic default print is unvowelled; vowelling is used mainly in reading instruction, religious and literary texts; some researchers argue the benefit is limited to early grades and weak readers (Frontiers in Psychology 2018, doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00356; https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00356/full ; search summary only). Voweled and unvoweled word reading across grades 2-8 shows an effect of linguistic distance (Scientific Studies of Reading; https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/the-impact-of-diglossia-on-voweled-and-unvoweled-word-reading-in-/ ; summary only). Product implication (inference): for 6-8 text on screen, show full tashkeel; for 2-5, text is read aloud so diacritics matter only for the narrator voice.
- No study found on graded readers or children's books with tashkeel: gap.
### Hebrew
- Beginning readers learn with pointed (niqqud) text, a "consistent, phonologically well-specified script"; the hard step is the transition to unpointed text, which begins about grade 3 (Journal of Learning Disabilities 2017, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022219417718198 ; Shany, Bar-On & Katzir 2012, Reading and Writing, https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/reading-different-orthographic-structures-in-the-shallow-pointed-/ ; both via search summary). Modern Israeli print uses niqqud mainly in children's texts, poetry, dictionaries (Wikipedia, weak source).
- Implication (inference): Hebrew text for 6-8 fully pointed; do not switch to unpointed before grade 3. No Israeli publisher conventions found for age bands (Keter, Kinneret etc. not checked): gap.
### Danish
- Nothing authoritative found on Danish age-band conventions. Curriculum is institutional (vuggestue, børnehave, børnehaveklasse) and sets goals for the environment, not the child (BUPL leaflet, above). A Danish retailer describes board-book style picture books for the youngest with few strong pages and recognisable everyday things (commercial, weak). One Danish study (via search) notes Danish picture-book illustration shifted from naturalistic to more abstract since the 1950s (Christensen 2003; not opened). The Danish "remser" (nursery rhymes) tradition could not be sourced: gap.
### English and cross-language (rhyme)
- Read, Macauley & Furay (2014), Frontiers in Psychology 5:149, "The Seuss boost: rhyme helps children retain words from shared storybook reading": 3-5 year olds remembered novel words better from rhymed than unrhymed stories, most when rhyme made the target word predictable; pausing before the target word to invite a prediction helped more than pausing after or reading straight (summary via search; https://scholarcommons.scu.edu/psych/285 ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00149/pdf ; paper not opened). Children learned nouns more easily than verbs. English-language study: transfer to Arabic, Hebrew and Danish rhyme is UNVERIFIED (rhyme in translation is rarely preserved, so write each language natively).

## I. Borders: evidence and recommendation (question 1)
Evidence FOR a boundary near age 4 (against the single "3-5" block):
- DM (England) separates Birth-3, 3-4 and Reception (4-5); EYFS statutory profile at the end of Reception (age 5). So England has a 4-5 band, and a 3-4 band, but also puts 3 with 4.
- CDC checklists are given at 2, 3, 4 and 5 years each (https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html) while the parenting-tips page merges 3-5 (so the owner's hypothesis follows the tips page).
- Reach Out and Read (from AAP Bright Futures): questions shift from what/where at 3 to why/how/"would you do that?" at 4, and "what happens next" and relating the story to life at 5.
- Wellman et al. 2001: understanding that others hold false beliefs arrives about age 4 (stories with a character who is mistaken, or a lie, need age 4+).
- DM: sentences of 4-6 words and "because" sentences around age 4; about 300 words and up to 5-word phrases around age 3.
Evidence FOR a boundary at 2 / 3: DM "Birth to three" ends at 3rd birthday; AAP/WHO start of any screen use at 18-24 months with co-viewing and WHO "no more than 1 hour" from age 2; CDC age-2 and age-3 checklists differ (two-word phrases vs back-and-forth conversation).
Evidence FOR a boundary at 5/6: school entry (Denmark børnehaveklasse about age 6, Israel compulsory kindergarten at 5 and grade 1 at 6, England Reception at 4-5 and Year 1 at 5-6); CDC treats 6-8 as one stage; "5-to-7 shift" (White 1965; Sameroff & Haith 1996 book, summary only; later view: a shift in tendencies of thought, not a wholesale reorganisation; https://psychology.iresearchnet.com/developmental-psychology/cognitive-development/five-to-seven-shift/ ). Fehr 2008: fairness preferences appear strongly only at 7-8.
Evidence against splitting 6-8: CDC keeps 6-8 together; no trusted source I found gives a clean 6/7 border. Within 6-8, reading level changes fast (pre-reader to independent reader about 6 to 8; Kaiser (secondary): many 8-year-olds enjoy reading).
RECOMMENDATION (my judgement, not a sourced fact):
1. Keep THREE groups, but cut them contiguous by completed years, 2-3 / 4-5 / 6-8, instead of 2-3 / 3-5 / 6-8 (the overlap at 3 is a design problem: a 3-year-old cannot handle a 5-year-old's story, and the evidence puts the step at 4).
2. Treat age 3 as the "stretch" year: in the app, the 2-3 band is written for 2.5-year-olds and the 4-5 band for 4.5-year-olds; a 3-year-old can be offered either, picked by the parent (use words understood: 3 about 300 words, 4-6 word sentences at 4).
3. Do not make a 1-2 band (AAP/WHO discourage screens before 18-24 months); 2-3 is a sensible youngest group. Product should say so on the first screen.
4. 6-8 is supported by CDC; consider an internal marker "early reader (6) vs independent reader (8)" for text size, tashkeel/niqqud and sentence length, not a fourth group.

## K. Milestone matrix per group (question 2), source beside each point
### Ages 2-3
- Language: about 50 words and two-word phrases towards age 2; about 300 words and up to 5-word phrases towards age 3 (DM, Communication and Language, section D1); CDC: two-word phrases by 2, two back-and-forth exchanges and who/what/where/why questions by 3, understood by others most of the time (https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html). Large normal variation (Fenson 1994, section F10).
- Sentences they understand: three-key-word instructions ("Can you wash dolly's face?") (DM); two-word phrases at 18 months (DM).
- Attention: chooses own activities, hard to direct, easily distracted; can switch task if you say their name (DM). Looks at the screen in bursts; attention depends on comprehensibility (secondary, section G). No sourced minutes.
- Memory: remembers favourite book text and "reads" along at 24-36 months (KidsHealth, hospital source, section G).
- Emotional: tantrums and "me do it"; starting to name feelings; calms after parent leaves within 10 minutes by 3 (DM; CDC3).
- Social: notices others hurt (2); joins other children (3); parallel and pretend play (CDC2, CDC3, DM).
- Moral: none stated by trusted sources at this age beyond rule-following and effortful control (DM); sharing mostly self-serving (F08, ages 3-8 sample).
- Numeracy/literacy: finger-number rhymes, "more/same", patterns (DM); points to pictures in books (CDC2); draws a circle (CDC3).
- Motor: kicks ball, runs, stairs, spoon (2); strings beads, puts on clothes, fork (3) (CDC2, CDC3).

- Language: sentences of 4-6 words, "because", past and future tense, answers why (DM 3-4); describes events in detail, retells stories, new vocabulary through the day, uses connectives (DM Reception). ## L. Twelve-line summary
1. Borders: 2-3 and 6-8 are well supported; the 3-5 block hides a real step at about age 4, so use 2-3 / 4-5 / 6-8 (England, CDC checklists, Reach Out and Read and false-belief research all point there). 3 is a stretch year.
2. England separates birth-3, 3-4, Reception; Denmark has no child-level age bands (goals are for the learning environment); Israel's source pages were not read.
3. Language: about 300 words and 5-word phrases at 3; 4-6-word, "because" sentences at 4; fluent retelling at 5; so 2-3 stories need picture-led, one-idea, repeated-phrase text; 4-5 can carry a simple plot with a problem and a feeling.
4. Screens: WHO and AAP agree on no screens before 18-24 months and at most 1 hour a day for 2-5, less is better, co-viewing; AAP's 2026 policy moves from limits toward quality, context and conversation. A film is a small part of the hour: make it short, finite, no autoplay, with a parent prompt.
5. No trusted source gives "minutes of attention by age"; the folk rule must not be used. Film lengths in this file are proposals to test.
6. Story norms: board books under 50-200 words, picture books at or under 500 words (publishing guide, single source), 24-32 pages; repetition and rhyme help toddlers predict; pauses before the rhyme word helped 3-5 year olds learn words (Seuss boost study).
7. Arabic: one 2024 study (139 children, about 5.7 y) found dialect-heavy stories understood best and MSA-only worst; graded move towards MSA works. So narrate in simple MSA with dialect-close wording and scaffold new MSA words.
8. Hebrew: pointed (niqqud) text for beginners, unpointed from about grade 3. Danish: little sourced; write natively, do not translate rhymes.
9. Strongest evidence of effect: shared reading links to reading and language (Mol & Bus 2011; dialogic reading adds expressive vocabulary, Mol 2008); SEL programmes help skills and a little achievement (Durlak 2011; Taylor 2017); kindergarten prosocial skills predict adult outcomes (Jones 2015); childhood self-control predicts adult health, wealth, crime (Moffitt 2011).
10. Moral stories: showing honesty rewarded works, showing the liar punished does not (Lee 2014): write behaviour stories with positive models, not threats.
11. Teaching goals: 31 / 36 / 30 listed with sources; the 4-5 list is best supported (England and CDC); the 6-8 list rests mostly on one CDC page and some goals (respect for elders, kindness to classmates, money) have no specific trusted source.
12. Biggest honesty warning: evidence for storybooks or short films changing child behaviour is thin; the big long-term studies are school SEL programmes and observational studies, so the product should pair each story with a parent or teacher activity.
